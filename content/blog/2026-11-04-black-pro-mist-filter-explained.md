**What does a Black Pro-Mist filter do?** It's a diffusion filter that makes the brightest parts of a picture glow softly into their surroundings, and it lowers the overall contrast a touch. Tiffen, which makes it, says it does that while keeping detail, which is what separates it from an old-fashioned soft-focus filter.

The look is suddenly everywhere. This autumn alone, Fujifilm announced an Instax film with the glow built into the emulsion, added a Soft Glow filter to its X half camera, and the iPhone 18 Pro gained a Glow texture. Here's what the effect actually is, and how to get a convincing version on a phone.

## What a Black Pro-Mist filter does

Tiffen's own product pages describe four things the filter does:

- **Tames the highlights.** It reduces the value of the highlights while slightly lowering the overall contrast.
- **Softens skin.** Tiffen says it's especially good at smoothing wrinkles and blemishes, which is why it's popular for portraits and beauty work.
- **Keeps detail.** Unlike a traditional diffusion or soft-focus filter, the Pro-Mist range causes little loss of detail across the image.
- **Spares the shadows.** Compared with Tiffen's regular Pro-Mist, the Black version lowers contrast with less lightening of the shadows, for a more delicate effect.

That last point is the whole reason the "black" version exists. Regular mist filters can wash blacks out to grey. Black Pro-Mist keeps the dark parts dark and puts the glow where the light is.

## Which strength to choose

Black Pro-Mist comes in densities written as fractions. Higher numbers mean a stronger effect.

| Grade | What Tiffen says it does |
| --- | --- |
| 1/8 | The mildest grade |
| 1/4 | More contrast and highlight reduction than 1/8, less than 1/2 |
| 1/2 | Stronger again |
| 1 and up | More contrast and highlight reduction again |

Tiffen describes 1/4 as still allowing a "soft, pastel-like quality of light", and its range goes well beyond 1/2: its UK site lists the Warm Black Pro-Mist up to grade 3. For everyday photos, start low. Strong mist looks great in a music video and odd on a birthday cake.

## Bloom, halation and diffusion: the naming mess

Tiffen's UK listing describes the filter's spill as a kind of halation. That's a looser use of the word than film photographers mean. In film photography, halation means something specific: a red-orange halo caused by light bouncing back through the film itself, as we explain in [what halation is](/blog/what-is-halation).

What a diffusion filter makes is closer to what photographers call bloom: a softer glow where bright areas spill into their surroundings, caused by light scattering in the filter. The quick tell:

- **Diffusion glow** mostly takes the colour of the light. A white lamp glows white, a warm lamp glows warm, though Tiffen notes a warmer spill at heavier grades.
- **Halation** leans red-orange and hugs the edge of the brightest highlights.

Real night scenes on film can have both. A digital look that confuses them is usually the giveaway.

## The diffusion look is suddenly everywhere

The glow has moved from filters into film, cameras and apps:

- **Instax SQUARE Soft Haze.** Announced by Fujifilm on 27 October alongside the instax SQUARE 2 camera, this square-format film gets soft light effects and warm colour tones from a newly developed emulsion. The camera reaches Japan on 13 November. (If you're choosing a format, here's [how Instax and Polaroid compare](/blog/instax-vs-polaroid-film).)
- **Fujifilm X half Soft Glow.** Firmware 2.00, released on 30 September, adds a Soft Glow filter that softens tone in the highlights to enhance the glow.
- **iPhone 18 Pro Glow texture.** Photographic Styles on iPhone 18 Pro models offer Soft Skin, Glow and Film textures, plus a Grain control.
- **Snapseed Bloom.** Google's free editor includes a Bloom tool for a soft diffusion around light sources, alongside a Halation tool, and it now has [a free film camera](/blog/snapseed-camera-android) too.
- **MARS.** This iPhone black and white app is built around a Glow control, which its maker describes as how much the light overflows. It's part of [the black and white film look](/blog/black-and-white-film-look) it's chasing.

## Filter or software on a phone?

You have two routes on a phone.

**A real filter.** Clip-on and magnetic mist filters are made for phones. Tilta, for example, sells a magnetic Black Mist filter for iPhone in 1/4 and 1/2 strengths. The glow is optical, so you see it live and it reacts to real light. The catch: phone HDR pulls bright highlights down to save detail, which can tone down the very glow you paid for. We explain that in [why phone photos look overprocessed](/blog/why-phone-photos-look-overprocessed).

**Software.** A glow effect in an app or the camera itself is free and adjustable. It's only as good as its model of light, though. Use this checklist to judge one:

1. **The glow scales with brightness.** A bare bulb should glow far more than a lit window, and mid-tones shouldn't glow at all.
2. **The shadows stay dark.** If the whole frame goes milky, it's a contrast slider, not a mist.
3. **Detail survives underneath.** The glow should sit around edges, not blur them.
4. **The colour follows the light.** A uniform orange halo on everything is halation done badly, not diffusion.
5. **It's gentle in daylight.** Like the filter, the effect is easiest to see at night or against bright backlight.
