For years, if you wanted a **black and white disposable camera**, your options were thin. Fujifilm's QuickSnap, the line it calls the world's first single-use camera, only ever came loaded with colour film. That changed this autumn with the QuickSnap Black and White, on sale from September 2026.

The best part is practical: Fujifilm says it develops in ordinary colour negative chemistry, so you don't need to hunt for a black and white lab. Here's what's inside, how to get good results, and how it compares with other ways to shoot black and white.

## What's in the QuickSnap Black and White

Fujifilm announced it on 1 July, for the QuickSnap line's 40th anniversary. DPReview's launch coverage filled in the specs:

| | QuickSnap Black and White |
| --- | --- |
| Film | ISO 400 black and white negative, emulsion not named |
| Exposures | 27 |
| Lens | 32mm plastic lens, fixed focus, f/10 |
| Shutter | 1/140 second |
| Flash | Built in, about 3 metres, with an on/off switch |
| Developing | Colour negative chemistry: C-41 or CN-16 |
| Price | $23 in the US at launch, per DPReview |

Fujifilm describes the film as having sharp images and smooth grain. It hasn't said what the film actually is. DPReview notes that Fujifilm's only black and white film still in production, Neopan 100 Acros II, is a 100-speed stock, so this 400-speed film is something else.

## Why the C-41 part matters

Most black and white film needs its own developing process. Ilford's beginners' guide spells it out: standard black and white films need a specialist lab, while its XP2 Super, a black and white film built for colour chemistry, can go to almost any lab, supermarket or chemist that develops film.

The QuickSnap Black and White works the same way. Fujifilm says it's compatible with colour negative processing chemicals, CN-16 and C-41, so any lab that handles colour film can take it. For a disposable camera, that's the whole point: drop it off with your colour rolls and pick up black and white prints.

If you'd rather shoot black and white in your own camera with the same easy developing, Ilford's XP2 Super is the 35mm film to look for.

## How to get good results

A disposable camera does very little for you, so a few habits matter:

1. **Give it light.** An f/10 aperture and a fixed 1/140 second shutter are made for daylight. Outdoors in the day is where it shines.
2. **Use the flash indoors.** It reaches about 3 metres, so keep people within a few steps. Switch it off for daylight scenes.
3. **Don't get too close.** Fixed-focus lenses aren't made for close-ups. Step back and let the scene breathe.
4. **Think in light and shape.** Without colour to lean on, side light, shadows and silhouettes do the work. We cover this in [the black and white film look](/blog/black-and-white-film-look).
5. **Make 27 count.** You can't delete anything. That's part of the charm.

Grain will be part of every frame, and that's the point. It's the real structure of the image, not a texture added later, as we explain in [film grain vs digital noise](/blog/film-grain-vs-digital-noise).

## Black and White or Active?

Fujifilm launched a second QuickSnap on the same day, and it's worth knowing the difference before you buy:

| | QuickSnap Black and White | QuickSnap Active |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Film | ISO 400 black and white | ISO 800 colour negative |
| Flash | Yes, switchable | No |
| Waterproof | No | Yes, to 10 metres |
| Exposures | 27 | 27 |
| US price at launch (DPReview) | $23 | $25 |

Both develop in standard colour chemistry. The Active is the one for the beach, the pool or the ski slope. The Black and White is the one for streets, faces and evenings, thanks to its flash.

## What it costs per frame

At $23 for 27 exposures, the camera works out to roughly 85 cents a shot before developing and scanning, which vary a lot by lab. That's cheaper per frame than instant film, where a Polaroid costs over £2 a shot on Polaroid's own UK store, as we found in our [Instax vs Polaroid comparison](/blog/instax-vs-polaroid-film).

## Forty years of QuickSnap

Fujifilm introduced the QuickSnap in July 1986, and says it has sold more than 1.7 billion of them. The company says the appeal still resonates with younger photographers in particular: not knowing how a photo will turn out until it's developed.

The new model fits a wider pattern this year, from Fujifilm's Instax investment to Kodak's growing materials and film unit, which we pull together in [is film photography making a comeback?](/blog/is-film-photography-making-a-comeback).
