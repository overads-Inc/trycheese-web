**Chromagraph Camera** is the most interesting phone camera app of the autumn. It's a $12.99 iPhone app from director Rian Johnson and his longtime cinematographer Steve Yedlin, and it replaces the iPhone's own processing with the colour science Yedlin uses on the Knives Out films.

It launched on 28 October and, as Fast Company reported, went straight to the top of the App Store's paid Photo & Video chart. Here's what it actually does, why it matters for anyone who wants phone photos to feel like film, and how it compares with the other options.

## What is Chromagraph Camera?

Johnson asked Yedlin to build his ideal iPhone stills camera, and Yedlin made it with colour science programmer Eric Cameron. They built it for themselves first, then released it publicly. Here's what the App Store listing and launch coverage describe:

- **It starts from raw.** The app takes the iPhone's raw image data and builds the picture its own way. The listing is blunt: it's not a filter laid over the usual processing.
- **It skips Apple's look.** Johnson's pitch, as quoted by Fast Company, is that it bypasses the iPhone's look processing.
- **The look is opinionated.** Yedlin told Newsshooter the rendering covers tonality, colour, grain and halation, tuned to what he, Johnson and Cameron like.
- **It's stills only.** No video, and on some older iPhones not every camera supports the raw capture it needs.
- **It's a one-off $12.99.** Fast Company notes there's no free trial and no subscription.

## The big idea: you can build a film look

Yedlin is well known in film-making circles for colour science. As Fast Company explains, his 2015 Display Prep Demo argued that a filmic look isn't locked inside celluloid or a particular camera. It can be built by transforming raw digital sensor data, and film-makers should author their own looks: grain, tonal roll-off, halation.

That's the same idea we've been unpacking on this journal. A film look isn't one effect. It's a set of traits that work together, from [how highlights roll off](/blog/what-makes-photos-look-like-film) to [how grain differs from noise](/blog/film-grain-vs-digital-noise) and [where halation belongs](/blog/what-is-halation). Chromagraph is a well-funded proof that the approach works on a phone, if you start early enough in the pipeline.

The "start early" part matters. A look laid over an already-processed iPhone photo inherits its HDR and sharpening, which is why so many film filters fall flat. We explain the problem in [why phone photos look overprocessed](/blog/why-phone-photos-look-overprocessed).

## No live preview, on purpose

The detail we like most is in the App Store listing. You frame through the viewfinder like any camera, and see the finished look only after the image is processed. As the listing puts it, that's how a film camera works.

The gallery then keeps the full-quality raw files and applies the look on top, with controls for colour, tone and texture. You can export JPEG or HEIC files with the look baked in, or switch on auto-export to send every shot to your photo library.

| Snapseed Camera | iPhone and Android | Free | Yes | Yes |

| iPhone Photographic Styles | iPhone (Film texture on 18 Pro) | Built in | Yes | Yes, in Photos |
| trycheese | iPhone and Android | Free at launch | No | No, it's baked in |

Snapseed's camera shows its film styles live and keeps them editable; here's our guide to [Snapseed Camera on Android](/blog/snapseed-camera-android). MARS, the black and white app PetaPixel covered last month, develops each frame live. Apple's own Photographic Styles let you preview a style before you shoot, and the iPhone 18 Pro adds Film texture and Grain controls.

Reviewers are already comparing them. 9to5Mac's Michael Burkhardt called Apple's new film texture a bit exaggerated, and said Chromagraph feels close to perfect. That's one person's taste, but it's a telling one.

## Should you buy it?

If you shoot stills on an iPhone and want one strong, consistent look without editing every photo, Chromagraph is built for exactly that. We haven't tested it ourselves, so here's what to know from the listing and coverage before you pay:

- **It's stills only.** You'll still need another camera app for video.
- **The look is deliberately opinionated.** You can adjust colour, tone and texture, but within Yedlin's style.
- **Photos live in its own gallery first.** Switch on auto-export if you want every shot in your photo library straight away.
- **There's no free trial.** Look at plenty of sample photos before spending $12.99.

## What about Android?

Chromagraph is listed on the App Store only, for iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch, and we found no announced Android version. If you're on Android, the free route is Snapseed Camera, or the step-by-step recipe in our guide to [a film look on a Samsung Galaxy](/blog/film-look-samsung-galaxy).

## Where trycheese fits

Chromagraph and trycheese agree on more than they differ. Both skip the live preview, so you watch the scene instead of a filter. Both build the look into the image rather than painting it on top. The difference is the ending: Chromagraph keeps the raw file so you can keep adjusting, while trycheese's [film looks](/#looks) are baked in at the shutter, and there's [no live filter preview](/#in-the-moment) and no editing step after.

And trycheese is coming to Android as well as iPhone. It isn't out yet, and it'll be free at launch. In the meantime, if you're on iPhone and curious, Chromagraph is a fascinating look at what a cinematographer thinks a phone photo should be.
