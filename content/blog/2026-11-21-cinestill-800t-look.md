The **CineStill 800T look** is one of the most recognisable in photography: clean colour under streetlights, a red glow hugging every bright lamp, and grain you can see. This autumn it got a very big stage. In a 6 November post, CineStill describes how NBC partnered with it to document the 78th Primetime Emmy Awards, from the red carpet to backstage, entirely on 800T, with the film developed and scanned on site.

You don't need a pop-up darkroom to borrow the look, though. Here's what actually makes 800T look the way it does, why one filter can't copy it, and the honest ways to get close on iPhone and Android.

## What is CineStill 800T?

It's a colour negative film from CineStill, sold in 35mm and 120. According to CineStill's product page:

- **It's built from motion picture technology.** The emulsion comes from cinema film, made safe for ordinary C-41 lab processing without the remjet backing that movie film normally carries.
- **It's tungsten balanced.** It's rated EI 800 under 3200K light, the warm colour of incandescent bulbs.
- **It can be pushed.** CineStill says it can be push processed up to ISO 3200.
- **It's in demand.** A 36-exposure roll of 35mm was $17.99 on CineStill's US store when we checked, limited to 10 per customer because of supply.

CineStill says photographers have called it simply "800T" since 2013.

## What makes the 800T look

Four things, and only one of them is easy to fake.

1. **Tungsten colour.** CineStill explains that tungsten light emits very little blue, so daylight film turns orange under it. 800T has extra blue sensitivity to compensate. Under lamps it looks fairly natural. In daylight or shade it goes cool and blue, unless you use an orange 85 filter.
2. **Red halation.** Bright points of light pick up a red glow, more than on almost any other colour film.
3. **Speed and grain.** It's an 800-speed film, so grain is part of every frame.
4. **Night scenes.** CineStill's own list of ideal situations is incandescent light, candle light, fluorescent light and mixed light. The look is as much about where you point the camera as about the film.

CineStill also notes that it's popular to shoot 800T in daylight with no filter, for a cool look. That blue cast, plus the glow, is what most people mean by "the 800T look".

## Why halation is the hard part

Most colour films have an anti-halation layer that stops light bouncing back through the emulsion. On motion picture film, the remjet backing does that job, along with protecting the film from scratches and static. CineStill's FAQ explains that its films are made without this layer, so light reflects inside the film base, or off the camera's pressure plate, and glows on the red-sensitive layer in overexposed highlights. We go deeper on the physics in [what halation is](/blog/what-is-halation).

CineStill's guide says you'll see it most in strongly backlit scenes, strong window light and frames with bright points of light, like Christmas lights. It adds that medium format film and longer lenses reduce the effect.

That's why the look is so hard to copy digitally. The Phoblographer's Chris Gampat put it bluntly in 2021: the look "isn't possible without lots of post-production." He suggests a 3200K tungsten white balance gets you closer, but says that mostly gives you the look of a generic tungsten film, not 800T's glow.

## 800T on film vs on a phone

| 800T trait | What causes it on film | Phone equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cool, blue cast | Tungsten balance (3200K) shot in daylight or shade | Lower warmth, or a tungsten white balance |
| Natural colour under lamps | Extra blue sensitivity | Pull warmth down under warm lights |
| Red glow around lights | No anti-halation layer | A halation tool, on the brightest lights only |
| Visible grain | 800-speed film | Fine grain that changes with tone |
| Night mood | Low, artificial light | Turn off heavy night processing |

## How to get the CineStill 800T look on your phone

1. **Pick the right scene.** Go out after dark: neon, shop windows, streetlights, a bar lit by bulbs. Bright lights in the frame give halation something to grab. Our guide to [night photos on your phone](/blog/night-photos-on-phone) covers when to turn off Night mode so the shadows stay dark.
2. **Cool the colour.** On iPhone, Apple's Photos app lets you adjust warmth and tint after you shoot. Drag warmth down until warm lamps look closer to white and the shadows tip towards blue. On a Galaxy, Samsung's Expert RAW app gives you manual white balance, which we cover in [our Samsung film look guide](/blog/film-look-samsung-galaxy).
3. **Add halation, carefully.** Lightroom's [Glow tool has a Halation style](/blog/lightroom-glow-halation) on iPhone and Android, and Snapseed has a Halation tool too, as we explain in [our Snapseed Camera guide](/blog/snapseed-camera-android). Keep it to the brightest lights. If everything glows, that's bloom, the look of [a Black Pro-Mist filter](/blog/black-pro-mist-filter-explained), not 800T.
4. **Add grain last.** Fine grain that's stronger in the shadows reads as film. Coarse, identical noise reads as an overlay. Here's [how to tell the difference](/blog/film-grain-vs-digital-noise).

On newer iPhones, Apple's iOS 27 guide also lists Photographic Styles textures called Glow and Film, plus a Grain option. Apple doesn't say exactly how Glow renders, so check whether it gives a red halo around lights or a soft haze over everything before you rely on it.

## When to shoot the real thing

If you have a film camera, a roll of 800T is the only way to get every part of the look at once. A few tips from CineStill:

- **Rate it at 800 under tungsten light.** CineStill says that's where it performs best with normal C-41 processing.
- **In daylight, use an 85 filter or rate it at ISO 400 to 500**, unless you want the cool cast.
- **Any C-41 lab can develop it.** It doesn't need motion picture processing.
- **Use it fresh.** CineStill suggests keeping it in the fridge and shooting it within six months of purchase.

CineStill also warns about imitation "800T" films, which it says are repurposed motion picture stock that comes out fogged and slower than claimed. If you buy a film called 800T, check who made it.
