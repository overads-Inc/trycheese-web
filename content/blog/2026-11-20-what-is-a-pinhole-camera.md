**What is a pinhole camera?** It's the simplest camera there is: a light-tight box with a tiny hole instead of a lens. Light from the scene passes through the hole and paints an upside-down picture on film or photographic paper at the back. No glass, no focusing, no electronics.

It's also having a moment. This week brought news that Guinness World Records has recognised a pinhole camera under an inch on each side as the smallest film camera in the world. Here's how pinholes work, why their exposures take seconds instead of fractions of one, and what they can teach anyone who loves the film look.

## The world's smallest film camera

The record holder is the Pocket Obscura, built by Melbourne art teacher and photographer Luke Fazekas. As Amateur Photographer reported on 19 November:

- **It's tiny.** The 3D-printed body measures about 2.48 x 2.41 x 2.48cm, and Digital Camera World puts its weight at around 100g.
- **It's a box camera in miniature.** Fazekas based the design on the Kodak Box Brownie.
- **It shoots on paper.** It holds a tiny sheet of photographic paper, loaded in a darkroom, and you expose it by sliding up the front panel.
- **It's a new record.** Guinness didn't have the category before Fazekas asked. To qualify, he had to prove the camera worked and measure it to the nearest 0.1mm on video.

Digital Camera World reports that Fazekas has said a limited number will be available from December.

## How a pinhole camera works

The Worldwide Pinhole Photography Day FAQ explains it neatly. A lens gathers rays from each point in the scene and focuses them back to a point. A pinhole doesn't focus at all. It works as a centre of projection: a ray from any point in the scene passes through the hole and lands in exactly one place on the film. Add up all those rays and you get a picture.

That has two big consequences:

1. **Pinhole images are soft.** The hole isn't a perfect point, so a small bundle of rays gets through from each part of the scene, and some light bends at the edge of the hole. Pinhole photos are always a little soft.
2. **Everything is equally sharp.** With no focus, there's no depth of field limit. The softness is the same from a few feet away to the horizon. Only objects closer than the pinhole-to-film distance get softer still.

## How big should the pinhole be?

Smaller isn't always sharper, because a tiny hole bends light more. The FAQ gives a formula for the "optimum" size, meaning the sharpest result, though it notes the sharpest picture isn't always the best one:

- **Optimum diameter (millimetres)** = 0.03679 x the square root of the focal length in millimetres.
- **f-stop** = focal length divided by the diameter.

Here's what that means in practice, using the FAQ's own example and one of ours:

| Camera | Pinhole-to-paper distance | Optimum pinhole | f-stop |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Large box camera (FAQ example) | 6 inches | About 0.018 inches | About f/333, rounded to f/352 |
| Matchbox-sized camera (our example) | 20mm | About 0.17mm | About f/122, rounded to f/128 |

Our matchbox example is just an illustration of the maths, not the Pocket Obscura's real settings, which we haven't seen published.

## Why pinhole exposures take so long

Those f-stops are why pinhole photography is slow. The FAQ's example works it through: in bright sun, photographic paper behaves like a film of roughly ISO 6. The Sunny 16 rule puts the exposure at 1/6 second at f/16. Stepping that down nine stops to f/352 takes it to about 85 seconds, before you even correct for reciprocity failure, where long exposures need extra time.

Our 20mm example is faster, at about six stops below f/16, so roughly 11 seconds on paper in sun. That's still a world away from a phone. The iPhone 18 Pro's main camera opens to f/1.48, as we covered in [what its variable aperture really changes](/blog/iphone-18-pro-variable-aperture). f/128 lets in about 13 stops less light than that, and f/352 about 16 stops less.

That's why pinhole cameras live on tripods, windowsills and walls, and why moving people turn into ghosts.

## How to try pinhole photography

You don't need a world record to start. The FAQ makes a few practical points:

- **Almost any light-tight container works.** People have made pinhole cameras from boxes, tins and even rooms.
- **You can use your film camera.** Fit a pinhole to a drilled body cap on an interchangeable-lens camera, and you can shoot regular film and send it to a lab.
- **You don't strictly need a viewfinder.** The camera works whether or not you know exactly what it's seeing.
