**How to take night photos on your phone** that still look like night comes down to one thing: stopping the camera from trying so hard. Modern night modes are brilliant at turning darkness into a bright, clean picture. The trouble is that night isn't bright or clean.

Here's why phone night shots often look fake, and five steps to keep the dark, the glow and the grain that make night feel like night.

## Why phone night photos look fake

Every major phone treats darkness as a problem to solve. Apple says Night mode automatically brightens photos and captures more detail in low light. Samsung says its Night mode uses multi-frame processing to combine 30 images into one photo. Pixel's Night Sight switches itself on in low light.

The results are sharp and bright, but they have tells:

- **The sky goes grey.** Shadows get lifted until nothing is truly black.
- **Lights lose their glow.** Bright signs and streetlamps are pulled down to keep detail, the same trade-off we cover in [why phone photos look overprocessed](/blog/why-phone-photos-look-overprocessed).
- **Texture turns waxy.** Noise reduction scrubs away speckle, and Cambridge in Colour warns that removing luminance noise completely leaves images looking plasticky.

None of that is wrong for a receipt or a menu. For a night out, it drains the mood.

## Step 1: Decide how bright night should be

Start by turning the automation down, or off, when you want atmosphere:

| Phone | How to turn it down |
| --- | --- |
| iPhone | Tap the Night mode button to switch it off, or choose an exposure option in Night mode |
| Pixel | Tap Night Sight on the right in Photo mode, drag the slider from Max to Off, then set More light to None |
| Galaxy | Night is a separate mode under MORE, so you choose when to use it. Low-light processing in Photo mode varies by model |

You don't have to give up night modes entirely. Shoot the same scene both ways and keep the one that looks like what you saw.

## Step 2: Expose for the lights

Tap on the brightest light you care about, then drag the exposure down until it stops blowing out. Let the shadows go black. At night, the dark parts of the frame are where the mood lives.

A darker exposure has a second benefit: the camera can use a faster shutter speed, so there's less blur from shaky hands.

## Step 3: Keep the phone still

Night photos are long photos, even when they don't feel like it. Apple recommends a tripod for more detailed Night mode shots, and shows crosshairs when it detects movement so you can line them up. Google says to hold still for a few seconds while Night Sight captures.

No tripod? Wedge the phone against a wall, a railing or a table. It's the cheapest upgrade in night photography.

## Step 4: Find the light, and let it glow

The best night photos are built around light sources: a streetlamp, a neon sign, a lit window, reflections on a wet road. Put them against a dark background and they become the subject.

That's also where film's most famous night trait shows up. Around bright lights on film you get halation, the red-orange glow made famous by CineStill 800T, which we explain in [what halation is](/blog/what-is-halation). If you'd rather have a softer white glow, that's bloom, the effect [a Black Pro-Mist filter](/blog/black-pro-mist-filter-explained) adds. Either way, a little glow is what makes a light feel like a light.

## Step 5: Let grain be grain

Some noise at night is unavoidable. Canon explains why: shadows receive less light, so there's less image information compared with the noise, and brightening them later only reveals noise that was already there.

You can fight that with more light. A faster lens helps, which is why Apple pitches the iPhone 18 Pro's f/1.48 setting for low light, as we explain in [what its variable aperture really changes](/blog/iphone-18-pro-variable-aperture). But you can also make peace with it. Fine, even texture reads as grain and feels like film. Smearing it away reads as fake. Here's [how film grain differs from digital noise](/blog/film-grain-vs-digital-noise).

## When Night mode is the right call

Night modes aren't the enemy. They're the right tool when you need to see detail rather than feel the mood: reading a sign, finding a friend's face in a dark bar, or a group shot where everyone needs to be recognisable.

They're also how phones photograph stars. Pixel's Astrophotography mode, inside Night Sight, works best 45 to 90 minutes after sunset and away from city lights, according to Google, with the phone set down on a stand or tripod rather than held. The trick is choosing on purpose, instead of letting the camera choose for you.

## What a film night photo looks like

If you want a target to aim for, think of a night street shot on film: deep blacks, bright lights with a halo, visible grain, and colour that leans warm or cool depending on the light. It isn't evenly lit, and it doesn't show every brick. It shows how the night felt.

Use that as your test. If your phone's version looks like a well-lit afternoon with a dark blue sky, it's trying too hard.
