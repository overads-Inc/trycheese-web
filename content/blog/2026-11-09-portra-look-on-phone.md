Everyone wants the **Portra look on a phone**: the warm, flattering colour of Kodak's most famous portrait film, without buying a roll. You can get close. But first, it helps to know what you're actually chasing, because even the people selling Portra-style products don't agree on what Portra looks like.

Here's what the film really does, why a piece of glass can't make it, and the honest routes on iPhone and Android.

## What does Portra actually look like?

Start with Kodak's own words. Its datasheet calls Portra 400 the world's finest-grain high-speed colour negative film, with spectacular skin tones and exceptional colour saturation across a wide range of lighting conditions. Kodak, which now sells the same emulsions as [Ektacolor Pro](/blog/ektacolor-pro-vs-portra), describes the 160 as exceptionally natural skin tones with soft, accurate colour, and the 800 as keeping natural skin tones in low light.

Now compare two very different takes:

- **PolarPro**, which sells a Portra-inspired lens filter, describes Portra as softened contrast, lifted shadows, slight warmth and a subtle magenta bias.
- **PetaPixel**, criticising that filter, said real Portra 400 shows significantly more contrast and saturation than PolarPro's samples.

Fuji X Weekly makes the point that settles it. One film can produce a whole range of looks, depending on which version you shoot, whether you over- or underexpose it, the light, how it's stored, how it's developed, and how it's scanned. There is no single "Portra look". There's a family of them.

So the useful question isn't "is this Portra?" It's "does this behave like colour negative film?"

## The Portra checklist

Whatever tool you use, these are the traits worth chasing:

1. **Skin first.** Natural, warm, flattering skin is Portra's whole reputation. Orange faces mean the look is overcooked.
2. **Soft highlights.** Colour negative film holds highlights longer than digital and lets them fade gently, as we explain in [what makes a photo look like film](/blog/what-makes-photos-look-like-film).
3. **Rich, not neon.** Kodak promises strong saturation, but greens and skies should still look real.
4. **Fine grain.** Portra is billed as very fine-grained, so a look with chunky grain is chasing a different film. Here's [how real grain differs from digital noise](/blog/film-grain-vs-digital-noise).
5. **Colour that responds, not a wash.** Skin, foliage and sky should shift differently, not all take on the same tint.

## Which Portra: 160, 400 or 800?

Portra comes in three speeds, and they don't look the same. Pick the one you're actually picturing before you pick a tool:

| Film | How Kodak describes it | Phone styles based on it | Try it for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Portra 160 | Exceptionally natural skin tones, soft and accurate colour | Snapseed KP2, Portra Cam P160 | Bright daylight and portraits |
| Portra 400 | Spectacular skin tones, exceptional saturation across a wide range of light | Snapseed KP1, Portra Cam P400 | The all-rounder |
| Portra 800 | Natural skin tones that hold up in low light | Portra Cam P800 | Evenings and indoors |

The descriptions are Kodak's, for the films now sold as Ektacolor Pro. The last column is our suggestion: slower film for bright light, faster film for dim scenes, the same as you'd load in a film camera.

## Why a filter can't make Portra

PolarPro's Portra filter, announced in February 2026, combines three things in one piece of glass: a warm colour tone, white mist diffusion and a chroma polarizer. PolarPro says it embodies Portra 400's colour profile. The internet disagreed.

PetaPixel compared the filter's samples with real Portra 400 scans and found them washed out by comparison. Fuji X Weekly took a middle line: a filter can help with the general aesthetic, but on its own it can't make a picture look like a specific film, and it depends heavily on the camera and settings behind it. The diffusion part is [the Black Pro-Mist idea](/blog/black-pro-mist-filter-explained) in a warmer form.

The lesson is the same on a phone. Colour and glow from a filter aren't enough. A convincing Portra look needs the tone curve, the colour response and the grain together.

## The honest ways to get close on a phone

**Snapseed Camera (free, iPhone and Android).** Google's Snapseed includes two Portra-based styles in its camera: KP1, based on Portra 400, and KP2, based on Portra 160. You shoot through them in real time and can change the look afterwards. Its Play Store listing promises no subscriptions, purchases, ads or watermarks. Here's our guide to [Snapseed Camera on Android](/blog/snapseed-camera-android).

**Samsung My Filters (Galaxy).** Galaxy phones can build a camera filter from any photo by mimicking its colours and style. Make one from a frame you shot on Portra yourself, and you've got a filter tuned to your own scans. The steps are in our [Samsung Galaxy film look guide](/blog/film-look-samsung-galaxy).

**A dedicated app.** On iPhone, Portra Cam offers P160, P400 and P800 among its five stocks. It's free to try with a 10-shot trial, with a one-time upgrade for the rest.

**Tone down the processing first.** Any Portra look painted over a heavily HDR'd photo will look flat. On iPhone, Pixel and Galaxy, start with the settings in [why phone photos look overprocessed](/blog/why-phone-photos-look-overprocessed).

Whatever you pick, test it the honest way: shoot a portrait in open shade and a sunlit scene, then put them next to real Portra scans you like. If the skin looks right in both, you're close.
